Topic 1 · Lesson 10

Sets and Venn diagrams

By the end of this lesson you can:
  • describe a set by listing it, or with a rule like {x: x is a natural number}
  • use the notation n(A), ℰ, A ∪ B and A ∩ B
  • place numbers in a Venn diagram
  • use counts to find how many are in each part of a Venn diagram

1. What is a set?

A set is a collection of things, called elements. We write a set inside curly brackets.

B = {a, b, c, …} lists the elements. The dots mean "and so on".

A = {x: x is a natural number} is read "A is the set of all x such that x is a natural number". The part after the colon is the rule.

n(A) is the number of elements in A.

ℰ is the universal set: everything we are choosing from.

Predict first: Let ℰ = {1, 2, 3, …, 20}. How many prime numbers are in ℰ: 6, 8 or 10?
Check my prediction

8. The primes up to 20 are 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17 and 19. Remember that 1 is not prime.

2. Union and intersection

A ∩ B (intersection) = the elements in both A and B.

A ∪ B (union) = the elements in A, or B, or both.

Predict first: A = even numbers and B = multiples of 3, both from 1 to 20. Is 6 in A ∩ B?
Check my prediction

Yes. 6 is even and it is a multiple of 3, so it is in both circles.

3. Counting with Venn diagrams

The numbers in the circles add up to the total: n(A ∪ B) = n(A) + n(B) − n(A ∩ B).

We take away n(A ∩ B) because the overlap was counted twice, once in A and once in B.

Anything in ℰ outside both circles is in neither set.
